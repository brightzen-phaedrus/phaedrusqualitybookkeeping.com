The foundation of everything. Bad bookkeeping means a bad model — no exceptions. That's why bookkeeping quality assessment is always step one of an [IFM](/phaedrus/content/knowledge-garden/ifm/) review.

BKQA asks a simple question: **is the underlying data clean enough to trust?** Concretely: are accounts reconciled, do bank balances match book balances, and are there stale unresolved transactions parked where nobody looks?

## The Common Failures

The same problems appear over and over across small-business books:

1. **Cash deposits not grossed up** — deposits recorded net of merchant fees and credits, understating both revenue and expenses
2. **Bill-pay blind spots** — payment platforms that obscure what was actually paid to which vendor
3. **Accrual reversals clouding the weekly picture** — timing entries that make weekly numbers unreadable
4. **Expenses hitting accrual accounts directly** — bypassing the categories where anyone would look for them
5. **Journal entries netting to zero** — technically balanced, informationally empty
6. **Reclassifications creating phantom transactions** — the same dollar appearing to move twice

None of these show up as "errors" in the accounting system. The books close. The reports print. And the signal is gone — which is what makes them dangerous.

## Hard Rules

Rules we hold across every set of books we touch:

- **Sub-accounts only.** Never post to parent accounts. Parent-account postings make category totals unauditable.
- **Payee names on every transaction.** Batch-accepting bank feeds without setting payees creates data that can't be analyzed by vendor — which means it can't be analyzed.
- **Understand the business before categorizing.** Categorization without context is guessing with confidence.

## Why It's Step One

See [Signal vs Noise](/phaedrus/content/knowledge-garden/signal-vs-noise/): clean books are the signal, and every failure above is noise that compounds downstream. A forecast built on unreconciled books isn't conservative or aggressive — it's fiction.
