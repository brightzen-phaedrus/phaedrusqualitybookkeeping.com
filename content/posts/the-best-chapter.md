Chapter 3: *Existential Questions*.

A sheet metal worker who spent one year at Saint John Fisher College bets his overconfident, straight-A, Eagle Scout son a hundred dollars that he'll get into Harvard.

The son says no way. His name isn't on any buildings. They don't even know anyone who has visited there.

The dad smiles. "Ok, John, I'll bet you $100 that you'll get in."

The son loses the bet.

Then comes the Harvard MBA essay — a 25-year-old writing the blueprint for his entire life. Management consulting, buying and selling companies, starting his own, writing a book. Every single thing came true. In order.

The only aspiration that hadn't happened yet was the book.

*So, he started writing.*

The chapter closes with three poems stacked under the heading *Practical Philosophical Truth*:

**Practical** — The father's accidental haiku: "You're never gonna / Make any real money just / Work'n for someone else."

**Philosophical** — Inspired by a descendant of the last Chief of the Comanche: "I am the father of my sons / I am the one who comes before."

**Truth** — The Boot Sequence: "Today is the best day ever. I am free to do whatever I want."

Three sources. Three registers. One nerve.

Chapter 3 is where the book stops being about philosophy and becomes a life. The dad saw something his son couldn't see about himself. The essay proved that writing your story down changes what happens next. And the three poems at the end show that wisdom doesn't care where it comes from — a factory worker, an ancestor, a friend.

That's the whole book in one chapter.
