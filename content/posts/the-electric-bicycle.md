I told a friend last week that using this thing feels like someone put me on an
electric bicycle and now I need my next fix.

That's the honest version. Not "AI is going to transform my business." Just the
plain physical sensation of pedaling like you always have and suddenly going
three times as far for the same effort — and then wanting to do it again
immediately. I'm a founder, not an engineer. I've spent years describing what I
wanted built and then waiting for someone to build it. Learning to use an agent
harness changed where I sit in that sentence.

I should say what I mean by *harness*, because the word sounds more technical
than the experience is. It's a window. I type what I want. The agent has root
access to a machine and to the cloud console, so it can actually *do* the
thing — not draft an email asking someone else to do the thing. It runs
sessions. It runs tasks on a schedule while I'm asleep. And here's the part that
took me a while to feel: it *remembers what it's done.* Every time it solves
something, that solution stays. The next conversation doesn't start cold.

The first week, I thought the win was speed. Of course I did — speed is the easy
thing to notice. I'd ask for something that used to take a contractor a week and
have it in an afternoon. That's the electric-bicycle hit, and it's real.

But speed isn't what kept me up rebuilding the thing. What got me was that the
work *compounds.* The first time the agent figured out how to read one of our
financial models, that was a task. The second time I needed it, I didn't ask
again — it already knew how. We'd written it down as an *ability*: a capability
the system keeps, documented and reusable, not a clever trick that worked once
and rotted in a folder. One session of work, and the system permanently knew how
to do something it couldn't do the day before.

That distinction — between a thing that got done and a thing the system can now
*do* — is the whole game. Most software gets built, used, and forgotten. The
script that worked that one time is gone the moment you need it slightly
differently. An ability doesn't go away. It's infrastructure. The feature solves
today's problem; the ability solves the category.

Once you feel that, you stop measuring the agent by how fast it types and start
measuring it the way you'd measure a good hire: does the work hold up, and does
the next time go better because this time was done right? That's a Quality
question, not a speed question, and it's the only one that's ever mattered here.

The strangest, best moment came when I realized I could hand an ability to
someone else. A colleague and I both run our own version of this. He can grant my
system access to his, and mine can reference his work, pull a capability across,
and use it. We're not trading code. We're trading *abilities* — the things each
of us has taught our system to do. He described his setup as "the architecture
to my brain." That's exactly right, and the wild part is that two brains can now
share parts.

I showed the window to my daughter and said the quiet thing out loud: this is
where work gets done in the future. Not because it's fast. Because it's the first
tool I've used where the effort I put in *stays* — where today's work is the
floor for tomorrow's instead of starting over every morning.

So if you're a founder eyeing this from the outside, here's the one piece of
advice I'd give. Don't go in chasing speed. You'll get it, and it'll feel great,
and it'll fool you into thinking that's the prize. The prize is durability. Build
the ability, not just the answer. Make the work compound.

Then go get your next fix. I'm not going to pretend I don't.
